[S1] Engadget — "OpenAI countersues Apple and accuses it of stealing trade secrets" (27 November 2026)
OpenAI countersues Apple and accuses it of stealing trade secrets
The suit suggests that the iPhone maker is trying to “compete unfairly by any means available.”
OpenAI has filed a major lawsuit against Apple that accuses the iPhone maker of stealing trade secrets, according to a report by Financial Times . The folks behind ChatGPT have also accused Apple of "coercing and threatening" suppliers who work with both companies. OpenAI says Apple is trying to "compete unfairly by any means available", which includes encouraging employees to store corporate files on personal devices and cloud storage accounts.
The complaint is filled with flowery language that suggests "Apple set quite a trap for employees" and that "bright young minds thought they would work in Cupertino. Instead they were joining Hotel California: you can check out any time you like, but you can never leave."
This follows a suit issued by Apple against OpenAI that accused the AI company of stealing secret trade information . This was filed after OpenAI acquired an entity called io , which is a studio led by Apple's former design chief Jony Ive. At that point, Apple called ChatGPT "rotten to its core." Now it looks like OpenAI is getting involved in a bit of tit for tat.
Apple has yet to comment on today's lawsuit, but it wasn't that long ago in which the two companies were partnered up to bring ChatGPT to iPhones . Now OpenAI is prepping its own line of hardware products that could directly compete with Apple.
To that end, the company has accused a former employee of returning to Apple and sharing details about product plans and suppliers . This is pretty much the exact same thing Apple accused OpenAI of doing in its suit.
That filing names several former Apple employees and says they "surreptitiously accessed and downloaded dozens of Apple's confidential hardware-related files, including voluminous, detailed information about unreleased products, engineering presentations, technical specifications and proprietary project data."
In any event, the courts will have to settle this whole thing. We'll keep you posted as these two suits make their way through the legal system.

[S2] WIRED — "A New Breed of Chip Runs AI Models Using Waves of Light" (27 November 2026)
As artificial intelligence becomes an unavoidable part of everyday life and models become ever more sophisticated, the cost of the energy required to run the technology is ballooning.
To rein in runaway costs, AI labs and cloud providers are trying to build new data centers in places with cheap and plentiful renewable energy— like Scandinavia . But logistical challenges, a scarcity of key components , and a swell of anti-data center protests are getting in the way.
An expanding field of hardware startups, among them German company Q.ANT, is coming at the problem from a different direction: by developing a new breed of chip— photonic processors —meant to minimize the amount of energy that AI models consume.
Whereas regular GPUs transmit and process data by shuffling electrons around, photonic processors use waves of light. Because light doesn’t generate heat as it travels and allows more data to be processed simultaneously, photonic processors supposedly consume less energy and require minimal cooling—one of the largest sources of cost for data centers.
The aim isn’t to replace GPUs with photonic processors, but to run them side-by-side. Whilst GPUs excel at grinding through masses of additions and multiplications—linear math—photonic processors are best suited to complex, nonlinear calculations.
Few photonic processors are running inside data centers today. But Q.ANT recently rolled out its chips to facilities operated by cloud company IONOS as part of a pilot program. It says that more than 30 teams of engineers are now developing AI models optimized for photonic computing.
Earlier this month, WIRED spoke with Michael Förtsch, founder and CEO of Q.ANT. We discussed the energy bottlenecks constraining AI development, how photonic processors differ from regular chips, and the challenges Q.ANT faces in trying to popularize a new class of hardware.

[S3] Engadget — "LA school district blocks Google's AI search features" (27 November 2026)
LA school district blocks Google's AI search features
The company has insisted that AI overviews and search are a package deal.
After Google spent the past month telling schools that its search engine and AI were too "deeply integrated" to be separated , one of the biggest school districts in the country ripped them apart nonetheless. The AP reports that Los Angeles Unified School Distict (LAUSD), which enrolls about half a million students, quietly disabled Google's AI Overviews on school-issued devices.
Although LAUSD didn't make a formal announcement, parents and students noticed the missing AI summaries in search results. That prompted a local advocacy group, Schools Beyond Screens LA, to ask parents to send video evidence. The group then received confirmation from the school district that AI Overviews were indeed disabled, albeit without details on the exact method used. (Third-party tools seem a likely candidate.)
The move came after a Nov. 13 meeting of LAUSD's AI committee, where several Schools Beyond Screens members spoke. A few days later, the AI summaries had vanished from school devices. The district describes the change as temporary and about keeping students' experience "age-appropriate and safe."
Earlier this month, Google walked back one policy on AI in schools that had sparked a backlash: switching on its Gemini chatbot by default for many K-12 students. (Google's AI tools will now be opt-in for students.) But at the same time, the company insisted that the connection between search and AI was a different story. Google VP & General Manager of Education, Chris Phillips, told The New York Times that "search and AI are deeply integrated," and that if a district blocked AI, students "will not have access to Search, and they'll see a message from their school that indicates it's not available."
If that sounds like Google throwing its weight around to justify its massive AI spending , well, some of the company's own workers might agree. Over 500 of them signed a petition earlier this month , asking their employer to reconsider the strict requirements.
If Google doesn't back down, it could be risking a significant loss of business. New York City, Los Angeles and Chicago are all considering switching search engines if it comes to that.

[S4] Hugging Face blog — "Taking Trainium3 to the Track: Generating Videos with LTX-2.5" (27 November 2026)
Taking Trainium3 to the Track: Generating Videos with LTX-2.5
Thanks to our partnership with AWS, we were lucky enough to get access to a Trainium3 machine ( trn3.48xlarge , 64 NeuronCores) for a month. We spent most of the month testing Hugging Face models on it, and getting more of them to work.
With five days left, I allowed myself a little fun. When you have a Ferrari, every now and then you want to take it out on the track. So I asked myself: what if I tried to generate videos?
I picked LTX-2.5 from Lightricks, which generates video and sound together. By the end, once the models were loaded, a 10-second clip with sound took 22 to 23 s to generate, on half of the machine. Here is the result.
Two goals
I had two goals. The first was to see how easy it is to generate videos with diffusers on Trainium3. Not the simple clips from the examples: I wanted to make short films from a script, closer to a real use case, like drafting a movie. The second was performance. I wanted to get close to what you get with CUDA, or with LTX's own API.
For CUDA, I found no real figure to compare against. The only one is LTX's claim of 6.8 s for a 10-second 720p clip on four B200 GPUs, with no detail on how it was measured. So I set CUDA aside and compared with LTX's online API instead, which LTX says makes a 10-second 1080p clip in 23.7 s. In the end, the setup matches it, and even beats it by about a second: 22 to 23 s for a 10-second clip at 2048×1024, once the models are loaded, on half of the machine. Not bad, considering how little time I had to optimize the pipelines.
How it works
The input is a film: a JSON list of shots, each with a name and a prompt, much like a storyboard. LTX-2.5 does better with long prompts, so each prompt is a paragraph on the style, the subject, the motion, the camera, the light and the sound. from : the shot starts from the last frames of an earlier shot. Starting from the earlier shot's last frame keeps a subject's look across a cut; starting from its last 25 frames (1 s) also continues the motion.

[S5] arXiv cs.AI — "How Much Effort Does an AI Code Reviewer Need? Evidence from Claude 5.5 Models on Plausible-but-Wrong Patches" (27 November 2026)
Computer Science > Artificial Intelligence
Title: How Much Effort Does an AI Code Reviewer Need? Evidence from Claude 5.5 Models on Plausible-but-Wrong Patches
Abstract: AI coding agents write code faster than people can review it, so teams add AI reviewers. Reasoning models let such a reviewer trade cost for diligence through an effort setting. We asked how much effort a reviewer needs to catch plausible-but-wrong patches, which pass a benchmark's tests but fail stronger ones. Claude Opus 5.5 (preregistered), Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5 (exploratory) each reviewed the same 90 agent-written Django patches from SWE-bench Verified at low, medium and high effort. By strengthened SWE-ABS tests, 55 patches were wrong and 35 correct. In every model, high effort accepted fewer wrong patches than low (false success .45 against .67 for Opus, .49 against .65 for Sonnet, .51 against .78 for Haiku), a supported difference for Opus and Haiku. For Opus, medium was non-inferior to high within 15 percentage points (difference .018, 95% CI [-.065, .108]) and cost 20% less, short of the preregistered 30%. For Sonnet and Haiku, medium cost 39% and 51% less than high but was not shown non-inferior. Opus at medium or high agreed best with the labels; Haiku at high cost about 8% as much per review as Opus at medium, accuracy difference unresolved. Exploratory coding showed that most rejections named the tested defect and that effort added such rejections. No setting is a strong gate: each accepted at least 45% of wrong patches. For 32 of the 55 wrong patches the issue may not require what the failing test checks; without them, neither accuracy hypothesis holds for any model.
