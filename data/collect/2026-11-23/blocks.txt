Meta ads is now available in AWS Quick
Marketers have never had more data to act on. Take an apparel brand that advertises on Meta technologies: campaign results live in Meta Ads Manager, briefs and creative assets in shared storage, and inventory, customer lifetime value, and in-store purchases in a data lake. Analysts export reports, merge spreadsheets, and rebuild the same deck each week, and the data ages before anyone acts on it. When the team does decide what to change, someone returns to Ads Manager to update campaigns, ad sets, budgets, and audiences by hand. As a result, teams judge campaigns on metrics alone, and the path from insight to action stays slow and manual.
Today, AWS Quick added a Meta ads AI connector. The connector gives Quick access to ads Model Context Protocol (MCP) server operations. Meta ads customers can use natural language to create, manage, and analyze campaigns within Quick, while staying connected to the Meta ad management network. Marketers can complete tasks with support from AI, such as pulling insights to understand ads performance, creating product catalogs, and editing existing or creating new campaigns. Quick can also combine Meta ad management data with first- and third-party data sources to create dashboards, documents, slide decks, and automated workflows.
“Meta ads AI connector through Quick gives our marketing teams one place to go from campaign planning to execution. It lets us act on business insights directly in our ads, without the handoffs and tool switching that slow campaigns down. With fal.ai’s image, audio, and video models in the same workflow, we’ll test more creative ideas and spend more time reaching customers.” — Tina Sang, Head of Marketing at fal

[S2] The Verge — "We haven’t seen the last of the major tech outages" (22 November 2026)
We haven’t seen the last of the major tech outages
Microsoft and the US government agree there are more outages to come.
Over the last week or so, we have seen a nationwide FAA ground stop, a multi-day Outlook outage that brought entire companies to a standstill, and a banking outage at JPMorgan Chase . But the consensus is that there are more to come . Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton told CNN :
“The American people should be prepared for a great deal of investment in upgrading our systems; they should be prepared for investment in cybersecurity, and they should be prepared that any time there is a step change in technology, a step change in both compute and applications, there will be bumps in the road.”
Microsoft President Brad Smith issued a similar warning about further outages to CBS , but sounded a much more ominous tone.
“I think we have to be prepared, frankly, for the coming months because this isn’t the type of thing that just gets done in a couple of weeks … The whole information infrastructure of the world is in effect being upgraded right now.”
While the outages over the last week were believed to be caused by cyberattacks, Smith said that AI has made it easier to find flaws , “and then you can either use that knowledge to patch the code, or if you’re a bad actor, you can use that knowledge to try to hack into systems,” he said.
The last few months have also seen an increase in attacks on government and commercial systems, including those at the USDA , JP Morgan , and supermarkets .
Whether outages are brought on by AI-assisted hackers or human error during the rush to patch an aging system , one thing seems certain: there will be more in the coming months.

[S3] WIRED — "Banks Have Had It Up to Here With Your AI Agent" (22 November 2026)
Wells Fargo is set to enact an updated agreement with millions of customers, specifically about AI agents , starting on Tuesday. The bank’s warning to early adopters could not be clearer. “Your use of any AI Agent is AT YOUR OWN RISK,” read Wells Fargo’s new terms .
AI agents, which automate online tasks and click on your behalf , have stumbled through 2026. They remain nascent technology, but a growing subset of power users latched onto the personal bots this year. Sometimes these early adopters enjoyed the efficient help of automation, and other times they publicly embarrassed themselves when the AI agents made major mistakes, like posting bank details for everyone at work to see.
Builders of these AI tools often market agents as the ultimate tool for shopping decisions, whether that’s putting together a grocery order, finding accommodations for a vacation, or discovering specific holiday gifts for your parents. I’ve used an agent to help shop for a new couch , comparing options within a specific budget and tracking price drops.
Some users value these agents so much that they are oblivious to the overarching security concerns , pairing account credentials and granting permissions for the agents to take sweeping actions without forethought. Although the segment of online transactions completed by these autonomous tools remains minuscule, US-based banking institutions are already sick of third-party AI agents.
It’s not just banks; AI agents are igniting turf wars around the web. Amazon, one of the largest online retailers, blocked Meta’s Muse, one of the most widely used AI agents, from making purchases on its platform. Travel companies are also pushing back—United recently announced that personal bots would be blocked from purchasing flights on your behalf.
As the end of the year approaches, multiple banks have enacted new policies around agents or sent warnings. In late October, JPMorgan Chase changed its terms to ban third-party agents that log in with your credentials. That same day, Bank of America warned its customers not to share login info with agents. Along with the announced change coming from Wells Fargo, Citibank and PNC have also changed their terms to include warnings about agents.

[S4] TechCrunch — "Signal restricts mobile screenshots as it confronts ‘existential threat’ of AI surveillance" (21 November 2026)
Signal restricts mobile screenshots as it confronts ‘existential threat’ of AI surveillance
Signal announced Friday that it’s limiting users’ ability to take screenshots within the secure messaging app — a response to what President Meredith Whittaker described as the “existential threat” of operating systems that integrate agentic AI.
Whittaker wrote that by giving AI agents the ability to “scan” the information on users’ screens, Windows, Android, and macOS/iOS “are undermining Signal’s ability to independently guarantee privacy.”
“Now, in service of AI agentic capabilities, your sensitive data is being captured, stored, and made accessible to whatever local database or remote server an OS-level AI agent is using at that moment,” she said. “All of this is outside of Signal’s control once the data has been captured, and imperils the privacy of all the participants in a conversation.”
Signal already sought to limit these risks by blocking screenshots of its Windows app by default. Now it’s adding similar restrictions in Android and iOS.
That said, users can disable this setting if they wish, which Whittaker said would “break our privacy guarantees.” She also admitted this is an “incomplete” solution, as it doesn’t “address other more sneaky ways AI agents could potentially access application data.”

[S5] OpenAI — "Perplexity and Intelligence" (22 November 2026)
Perplexity and Intelligence
By Jakub Pachocki, Chief Scientist at OpenAI
From afar, it might seem like modern AI is built similarly to how software has been built in previous decades: with a diverse set of techniques targeting specific capabilities. There are indeed a variety of methods, but rather than designing something from scratch, they are mostly harnessing the intelligence coming from a much simpler and less interpretable process: training a compressor (or, equivalently ⁠ (opens in a new window) , predictor) on a very large corpus of data. 1
I believe tracking the pace of progress on training better compressors is key to understanding the arc of advances in AI. In this post, I argue for establishing perplexity (or, equivalently, cross-entropy loss) as a metric to compare between model developers and inform shared safety standards. As a first step, I provide more transparency into pretraining progress at OpenAI. Publishing our internal perplexity measurements took some consideration; it gives competitors a clearer picture of where we stand, but that concern is outweighed by the urgency of establishing shared ⁠ (opens in a new window) safety measures across the industry. We at OpenAI hope for this to be a step towards building a shared scientific basis for future pacing efforts.
A simplified, but fundamentally informative, view of AI development is that first, one builds a large computer, then uses it to pretrain a model (compressor) on a large unstructured dataset, then applies a variety of techniques—like prompting, finetuning or reinforcement learning—in different contexts to elicit the latent capabilities of that model. Algorithmic progress can blur the distinction between training phases, but we can still separate the creation of the AI with hard-to-predict emergent capabilities (which I earlier dubbed “ an alien mind ⁠ ”) and the subsequent targeted elicitation.
The primary way we measure progress on this key “intelligence substrate” has remained remarkably constant over the years—we have been tracking the perplexity of the models on a held-out dataset. 2 , 3 Perplexity measures how well a model predicts the next token on average; lower perplexity means better predictions. The plot opening this blog post shows how this metric evolved for our models starting with GPT‑3.5.
