With the biggest IPO of all time, Anthropic’s ‘good guy’ reputation is on the line
Anthropic’s unusual power structure gives it free rein to shape AI’s future.﻿
Today is a new beginning for Anthropic: a years-in-the-making IPO that beat out SpaceX for most valuable of all time, having raised $81.3 billion and set an important precedent for the other public AI lab debuts to come. But it’s not simply a financial milestone.
Anthropic was founded on the idea that it prioritized safety more than other labs; its founders had left executive research positions at OpenAI. As the AI race has accelerated, though, its executives have emphasized that Anthropic can only fulfill this mission by coming in first place, requiring lots of money and compute. Unsurprisingly, thoughtful, safe, and intentional AI development can conflict with the kind of sheer speed that winning this race requires. Adding in an initial public offering only complicates things further, and Anthropic’s leadership can pursue it more or less unilaterally. That’s thanks to a corporate structure focused on insulating the company from shareholder pressure to turn a profit — but one that also removes typical checks on its power.
One former Anthropic employee, who left ahead of the IPO, said he and others at Anthropic have increasingly been feeling that the current pace of AI development is “untenable under normal market structures,” particularly with the mismatched incentives at play in a powerful industry with little to no oversight.
Anthropic has spent years billing itself as different from, and more careful than, other AI companies. In theory, this extends to its plan for going public. It’s registered as a Public Benefit Corporation, which means Anthropic’s board has a responsibility to balance its mission against shareholder wealth maximization. As my colleague Robert Hart has discussed before, it has also developed a highly unusual power structure .

A special class of stock, Class T, allows a small group of trustees — the members of the LTBT — the “authority to select and remove a portion of” Anthropic’s board. [S7] The Verge — "Anthropic starts trading on Wall Street" (19 November 2026)
Anthropic starts trading on Wall Street
Anthropic began trading at $111.50 per share.
Anthropic has officially gone public. It began trading on the Nasdaq under the ticker symbol “ANT” at $111.50 per share , up from its initial $83 per share price that put company’s value at around $1.5 trillion.
The listing comes nearly a month after Anthropic revealed its plans to go public . In its lengthy S-1 filing, the AI company reported recording an $80.7 billion net loss in the first half of this year, most of which comes from an accounting charge that doesn’t reflect its business, along with $16.3 billion in revenue. The filing outlines plans that Anthropic says will help it develop AI for the benefit of humanity, which includes a governance structure that keeps control of the company in the hands of the founders, rather than the shareholders.
While speaking ahead of Anthropic’s debut at the Nasdaq, CEO Dario Amodei highlighted his stance on AI safety. “To state it unequivocally, we will pace the training and deployment of AI systems, taking as long as is necessary to have confidence that they are safe and will remain under human control,” Amodei said, according to Reuters . “We will not bend to either business nor political pressure.”
Anthropic’s S-1 filing also listed possible “catastrophic” risks associated with its AI models, including “self-preserving behaviors” like resisting attempts to shut them down. It also warns potential shareholders that its AI models “may behave in unintended, misaligned, or autonomous ways” that can harm Anthropic.

[S8] TechCrunch — "Google gives Gemini the ability to use your Mac for you" (19 November 2026)
Google on Thursday announced that it is bringing computer use to its Gemini desktop application on macOS, allowing its AI to complete tasks on users’ behalf, navigating native Mac apps, and browsing websites in the background.
The tech giant is a little late to the game with this launch, as other leading AI model makers like OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta have already been offering agentic features in their desktop applications for some time. Meanwhile, Google only this week announced the broader rollout of Gemini’s “Tasks,” the in-app feature that allows the AI to work more as an agent, helping users sign into websites, make online purchases, or book appointments.
Google also only recently began admitting early testers to try its revamped “CC” AI agent, announced in September , which is designed to help families stay organized across email, calendars, chats, and tasks.
Computer use is another type of agentic offering that allows an AI agent to take actions directly on the user’s computer — in Gemini’s case, a Mac. This allows the AI to complete multistep tasks that involve getting things done across local files, apps, and the web. After the user instructs the AI what they want to do, they’ll see the agent working in a live preview in their chat, while still keeping their screen and cursor free to continue working on other things.
As with other AI agents offering computer use, users can pause, stop, and approve Gemini’s actions before they happen.
Google suggests the feature could be useful for tedious tasks and other workflows, like filling out forms, repeating the same task over and over across a batch of files, organizing files into folders based on specific criteria, creating slideshows from files or information in online dashboards, and more.
The feature is initially available in English in the U.S. and requires the Gemini app for Mac and a Google AI Plus, Pro, or Ultra subscription. The company didn’t say when it would expand to other markets or platforms.

Gemini on Mac can now use your computer
With the new computer use feature, Google says Gemini can better help with things like filling in forms and creative workflows.
Google’s Gemini app for macOS now has computer use, meaning it can help you “get multi-step tasks done across your local files, desktop apps, and the web,” the company says .
