Kagi, a small, Palo Alto, California-based software company that provides a subscription-based search engine as an alternative to Google, has launched a Gmail-like e-mail service. Dubbed Kagi Mail, it makes many of the same claims as the search engine product: privacy, personal data sovereignty, customization, and control.
Additionally, Kagi is rolling out a calendar as part of the mail service. Right now, both the email and calendar interfaces are web-only, but native Android and iOS apps are planned for the fourth quarter of next year.
The email interface, currently in public beta, is minimalist. It emphasizes keyboard commands, as well as a private and ad-free experience. (You can see more details in Kagi’s blog post about what they mean by private.) Other feature offerings include unlimited aliases and domains, an anti-lock-in policy for user data, 50GB of storage, rule-based filtering with Sieve, an MCP server, and several optional, off-by-default AI features, like chatting with an agent to search email history, do large-scale re-labeling, or define additional filtering rules.
Some major features are still missing, though. As noted, there are no native apps yet. Critically, there is no contacts address book, but that is also coming in 2027 (Q1 in this case) according to the roadmap.
Like the search engine, Kagi Mail is a paid service. It’s not included in the $5 per month basic Kagi plan, nor is it in the $10 per month unlimited search plan. It is, however, included in the $25 per month Kagi Ultimate subscription, which also offers frontier AI models.
If you want to get Kagi Mail without the $25 per month subscription, you can subscribe for $9 per month or $99 per year, and there’s a 60-day trial right now.

According to the company, Kagi Search has just over 75,000 paying subscribers. Compared to the number of people using Google Search or even Bing or DuckDuckGo, that’s not much. In an announcement on Wednesday , the FCC says it authorized SpaceX to deploy up to 1 million AI chip-equipped satellites as part of the Elon Musk-owned company’s “Starmind” initiative . With several others also pursuing the idea of putting AI data centers in space , including Google and Blue Origin, this is the first approval the agency has granted for a full “constellation” of AI satellites.
The FCC’s approval requires SpaceX to deploy at least one satellite by 2033, and meet the 1 million satellite milestone by 2040. The FCC also deferred SpaceX’s request to dispose of satellites located above 600km in space “graveyard” orbits , rather than having them burn up in the Earth’s atmosphere, saying the company hasn’t provided “sufficient information” about its disposal process plans.

[S8] WIRED — "Meta Refused to Share Muse for Testing, UK Financial Watchdog Complains" (26 November 2026)
Meta declined a suggestion from the UK’s financial regulator last week that they joint-test Muse using their sandbox, according to the watchdog’s head of innovation Colin Payne. It comes as regulators across the UK, including the competition and data watchdogs, warned Meta it needs to ensure its AI products comply with UK consumer protection laws.
“It’s another force of attack, agents hitting the streets, most of them coming from the US,” Payne, of the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), said Tuesday in a panel hosted by AI firm Whitespace. “We’ve got a number of entrants into the market. We want to see what they’re going to be doing in the market. I think it’s just reasonable,” he later explained to WIRED. Payne told WIRED the FCA had been hoping the organizations could work together to investigate what interactions might look like between the tool, marketed as a personal assistant, and a normal customer.
His organization has been experimenting with other firms as many, including Amazon , roll out AI shopping assistants, and other agentic tools in the UK.
The FCA especially wants to understand how payments will be made by agents—as multiple British retailers, including grocers Sainsbury’s, Marks & Spencer, and Ocado, department store Debenhams Group, and electronics seller Currys said they’d been working with Meta to smooth Muse’s access to their sites. Payne is concerned about potential unintended consequences of prompts, especially when a user has a low level of understanding of how the AI product works. “If an agent is acting autonomously, we want to make sure it’s in the interest of the consumer,” he says.
Payne did note the testing would have taken time to set up. “Normally you have a long period of time speaking to a firm and then you plan it over a period of weeks or months,” he explained, but Meta only made the FCA aware of its plans last month.
“We’ve engaged constructively with the appropriate regulatory authorities and will continue to do so,” Meta Spokesperson Matthew Pollard tells WIRED.
More than 10 million users have set up Muse in North America since it was launched in September. British watchdogs say they will be monitoring Muse’s rollout closely as they are bracing for significant uptake on their side of the pond, too, given that many Brits already use Meta’s other products , like Instagram, Facebook, and WhatsApp. “If it’s going to be equally popular in the UK, we need to be able to see what it’s going to do,” Payne says.

[S9] Ars Technica — "AI may soon change what it means to be a theoretical physicist" (25 November 2026)
About six months ago, Suvrat Raju, a professor of theoretical physics at the International Centre for Theoretical Sciences in Bengaluru, India, asked a frontier AI model to solve a physics problem. He didn’t ask because he needed to know the answer. He had actually solved this particular problem already. But he had never published the solution—which made it a perfect question to test how good AI was at physics.
LLMs have been trained on the whole Internet, so if a physics problem has been discussed online, it’s likely the model already knows about it and may have memorized the answer. So a fair test of AI capabilities needs to focus on problems that have never appeared online.
The model didn’t answer Raju’s problem correctly, but it did better than he expected—well, enough to get him thinking that the experiment should be repeated and formalized. With the help of a colleague, he set out to create a theoretical physics benchmark for AI.
They asked other physicists for some of their private gems—minor but real findings they’d never gotten around to cleaning up for public display. They were asking for real work, problems that take humans at the top of the field about a day to solve.
“Our contributors had to either sacrifice potential publication elsewhere, or else undertake a small research project just for us,” Raju wrote .
By the time all the questions and answers were ready, the AIs were, too. At a colloquium in September, Raju shared preliminary results showing that leading AI models could solve about 60 percent of the problems on their own. And by the time he and his colleagues finished the full set, the situation had advanced further: GPT-6 Astra could solve nearly 90 percent of the problems.
Curious to know where Astra was struggling on the rest, they gave the model the human-written solutions and asked it to figure out where it had gone wrong. To their surprise, Astra argued that it hadn’t made a mistake—the humans had. Astra identified subtle errors in the human-written answers. In reality, it answered 49 of 50 problems correctly. And the one problem Astra missed was something of a trick question.
Each correct answer had cost less than $3 of compute on average—“less than the price of coffee in Paris,” as Raju put it in a talk—and had come after a single human prompt.
In other words, Raju’s TheoPhysEval benchmark was saturated before it was released. When Raju found out, he didn’t sleep well for a couple of nights, out of a hard-to-disentangle blend of astonishment, excitement, and fear.
