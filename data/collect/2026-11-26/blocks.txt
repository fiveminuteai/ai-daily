Jensen Huang’s return as CEO to Nvidia hasn’t eased the scrutiny the company faces. The chipmaker still needs to address multiple inquiries into allegations that its advanced GPUs were smuggled into China by a customer, the Singapore-based data center operator Megaspeed, in violation of U.S. export rules designed to slow China’s development of frontier AI models.
The central question for investigators is what Nvidia knew about where those chips ended up and whether Huang’s affair with a Megaspeed executive gave the company reason to doubt Megaspeed’s assurances. Huang’s return doesn’t settle that. U.S. Attorney General Todd Blanche has suggested Nvidia isn’t a target of an investigation, but California’s attorney general and Congress are pressing ahead, and the Securities and Exchange Commission is reportedly investigating, too.
The U.S. government opened an investigation into Megaspeed in 2024 and requested information from Nvidia about its billions of dollars in sales to the company, concerned that some of the chips Megaspeed bought for data center projects in Malaysia were in fact being diverted to China. Singapore also began investigating similar allegations in 2025. Megaspeed has denied any wrongdoing.
Diversion of this kind would be a fairly routine issue for a company like Nvidia, whose in-demand chips are the targets of smugglers and resellers. When it comes to compliance and liability for export-control violations, Nvidia and other chip sellers need assurances that the end users of their chips are obeying the rules. However, they also cannot ignore other information that might call the reliability of those assurances into question, export-control attorneys told TechCrunch.
And that’s why Jensen Huang’s affair with Megaspeed executive Alice Huang was such a problem for Nvidia, likely even more so than her alleged role as a source for Chinese intelligence agencies. If Nvidia personnel, from lower-level employees up to the CEO, had reason to believe that statements from Megaspeed weren’t reliable, the attorneys said, that would increase the company’s legal risk.
“Government investigators likely would want to know, notwithstanding whatever formal assurances were exchanged between the parties, what anyone at Nvidia actually knew about the end use and end users of the chips,” Dan Fisher-Owens, an attorney at the firm Berliner Corcoran & Rowe, told TechCrunch last week.

[S3] TechCrunch — "Jensen Huang broke Nvidia’s code of conduct, and his employees may not forget it" (25 November 2026)
When Jensen Huang gave his first all-hands address since returning as CEO, there was an odd emphasis on Nvidia’s code of conduct. He admitted to violating the code, then emphasized that no one was exempt from it. But that “does not mean, the moment you violate a code of conduct, we walk you out the door,” Huang said, according to Wired . “Facts, circumstances, a lot of assessments” still matter. In other words, the code applies to everyone, but breaking it doesn’t automatically cost anyone their job, including, apparently, the CEO.
It’s an awkward stance for a company whose CEO has admitted to breaking its rules. A central part of modern human resources, a code of conduct can seem like a quasi-legal rulebook that lays out unacceptable behavior. But experts say most codes are better understood as a reflection of corporate culture. When an infraction goes unpunished, the main risk isn’t legal but cultural — that employees will feel embarrassed or even angry about the company they work for. That’s the risk Huang now faces at Nvidia.

What happens to your private conversations when an AI company goes bankrupt ? If it’s Character.ai, apparently, the plan is to auction them off to the highest bidder.
Now, a coalition of 42 state attorneys general is intervening in Character.ai’s bankruptcy proceedings in an effort to stop the chatbot maker from selling off sensitive user data, which includes the private conversations of millions of users.
The attorneys general involved in the matter have put out individual press releases , claiming that Character.ai’s dataset includes chat histories from more than 21 million U.S. accounts, more than 2.1 million of which belong to minors. In addition, the sale would involve other personal data, like IP addresses, the statements said.
Character.ai, which filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in October, previously told consumers that their personal information would not be sold, the statements also noted. ( In a blog post , Character.ai assured its users that their information would still be protected under its privacy policy.)
Only one anonymous bidder for the chat data has been disclosed so far: Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC (Smirks), which was set up as a Delaware company on November 13, 2026. The following day, Character.ai picked it as the base bid for the auction, scheduled for December 1, California Attorney General Rob Bonta noted . It is unclear what the buyer’s plans are for the chats.
The AGs are opposing this sale on the grounds that there are no protections for users — whoever buys the data can do as they like, including reselling the data without disclosure, they said. Buyers also won’t have to honor Character.ai’s privacy policy. Plus, Character.ai hasn’t provided the court with as much information as the bidders have been given about any potential safety or legal issues following the sale, the AGs claim.
In addition, some states, including California, Colorado, New York, and Connecticut, have enacted chatbot safety legislation that will go into effect on January 1, 2027, which could trigger further violations if the sale goes through.

[S6] TechCrunch — "Amazon and Meta reportedly in talks to lift blockade on AI agent Muse" (25 November 2026)
Amazon and Meta reportedly in talks to lift blockade on AI agent Muse
Amazon may be having a change of heart about Meta’s AI agent, Muse. The two tech giants are reportedly discussing a potential deal that would allow Meta’s popular AI agent to connect directly to Amazon’s store.
The talks would mark a significant reversal from just two months ago, when Muse users discovered they were unable to purchase products on Amazon and instead received an error message when attempting to check out.
According to a Wednesday report by Fortune , citing people familiar with the discussions, Amazon and Meta have held multiple rounds of talks about the matter. Two key issues remain under negotiation: how search results would be presented and how advertising would be handled under a potential agreement.
One of Amazon’s biggest concerns is how third-party shopping agents like Muse could affect its advertising business. In October, Amazon CEO Andy Jassy argued that few customers were making purchases through outside shopping agents.
Amazon may also have been waiting for Muse to mature before agreeing to integrate it with its marketplace. If Muse places the wrong order or creates a problem with a purchase, Amazon could ultimately be left dealing with frustrated customers and unhappy sellers.
Fortune reported that executives are concerned that third-party shopping agents could weaken Amazon’s direct relationship with consumers, according to people familiar with the matter.
Any potential agreement between Amazon and Meta would reportedly require approval from both companies’ CEOs, Fortune also reported.
Meta and Amazon have not yet responded to TechCrunch’s requests for comment.

[S9] Ars Technica — "Amazon drone crashes on garage after delivering shampoo, draws FAA probe" (24 November 2026)
An Amazon delivery drone that clipped a fiber-optic line before crashing onto a garage roof is now the subject of an investigation by the US Federal Aviation Administration. It’s the latest delivery drone mishap caught on video as Amazon and other companies are ramping up such aerial delivery operations across the United States.
The November 20 incident began with Jim Brennan and his wife, residents of Huntington Woods, Michigan, placing an order for shampoo and being surprised by a notification about the package arriving by drone within seven minutes, according to WXYZ 7 News Detroit . After the drone dropped the package into Brennan’s backyard, it lost a propeller when it caught on fiber-optic cable and floundered sideways before getting pinned between the neighbor’s garage roof and some power lines.
“Like a sick bird, it tried to go up, it tried to get out of here,” Brennan told WXYZ 7.
Amazon Prime Air currently operates a fleet of battery-powered MK30 drones that can deliver packages weighing up to five pounds and have onboard sense-and-avoid technology to avoid obstacles. However, the drone clearly failed to avoid the thin fiber-optic cable in this case—so hopefully the FAA investigation would take that into account along with Amazon delivery drone procedures.
The FAA preliminary accident and incident notification briefly describes the incident as “UAS struck a fiber cable, a residential garage, and became entangled in power lines during takeoff.” That notice was posted on November 23.
The National Transportation Safety Board is also gathering information about the incident but has not yet decided whether to open its own probe, according to CNBC . That US government agency holds primary responsibility for investigating aviation accidents and other transportation incidents.
Logan Coomes, the neighbor whose garage was struck by the drone, described an Amazon Prime Air team arriving within 10 minutes and coordinating with Detroit Edison workers to eventually remove the drone by early evening, in an interview with WXYZ 7.
The Amazon Prime Air team also reportedly told Coomes that the company would help with repairs to his garage roof, and gave the homeowner a $100 gift card on the spot. Amazon claimed that this was the first such delivery drone crash from its operations near Detroit.
